Overview & Scope

About SPIROBE

SPIROBE is a bio-inspired robotic tentacle designed to replicate the flexible bending, curling, and grasping movements of an octopus tentacle.

The project uses a spiral-shaped segmented mechanical structure combined with a cable-driven actuation mechanism. Geared DC motors control cable movement to produce the required bending and curling actions.

The system integrates mechanical design, 3D printing, electronics, and embedded programming to demonstrate an alternative approach to conventional rigid robotic grippers.

Engineering Concept

Our Solution

SPIROBE is a 3D-printed, cable-driven robotic tentacle that uses a spiral-shaped segmented structure to achieve flexible movement.

The tentacle consists of multiple specially designed segments arranged along a flexible structure. Cables run along the length of the tentacle and are connected to a motorized mechanism. Geared DC motors pull and release the cables, allowing the structure to bend and curl. An ESP32 microcontroller controls the motors, while a wireless web interface allows users to operate the tentacle.

01 / MECHANICAL

Flexible Segmented Structure

Specially engineered 3D-printed vertebrae arranged in a graduated series to distribute bending smoothly across the continuum without localized buckling.

02 / ACTUATION

Cable-Driven Movement

High-tensile synthetic cables routed through low-friction guide channels along the lateral margins of the vertebrae to transmit pulling forces.

03 / POWERTRAIN

Motorized Actuation

High-torque geared DC motors fitted with precision dual-groove spool winches to reliably wind and unwind the tendon cables in antagonist pairs.

04 / EMBEDDED

ESP32-Based Control

Dual-core 32-bit microcontroller running firmware to process user movement commands and generate directional PWM drive signals for the motors.

05 / TELEMETRY

Wireless Web-Based Operation

Onboard wireless access point hosting a responsive browser control interface for multi-device tetherless operation and bench testing.

06 / FABRICATION

3D-Printed Components

Engineered additive manufacturing using durable PLA/PETG filaments, delivering high strength-to-weight ratio and rapid design iteration.

Kinematics & Anatomy

Mechanical Architecture

The physical construction of SPIROBE translates the biomechanics of an octopus arm into an articulated, 3D-printed cable-tensioned structure.

Spiral-Inspired Segmented Structure

The tentacle geometry follows a logarithmic spiral distribution. Rather than identical vertebrae, each successive segment scales in chord length and articulation clearance. This geometric gradient ensures that when tension is applied, the distal tip initiates curling first, smoothly propagating inward to form an enveloping grasp.

Individual 3D-Printed Segments

Each vertebra is 3D printed with interlocking mechanical limits that constrain rotational degrees of freedom while preventing out-of-plane torsional twist. Integrated smooth eyelets guide the tendon cables without sharp abrasion corners.

Cable Routing Along the Structure

Dual cables pass through concentric channels along both sides of the tentacle. The lateral offset from the neutral bending axis creates a substantial moment arm ($r \cdot F$), converting linear tension directly into bending curvature.

Central Motor and Spool Mechanism

Actuators remain stationary at the chassis base. High-reduction N20 DC gearmotors drive dual-channel winches, pulling one cable while maintaining calibrated counter-tension on the opposing cable to preserve kinematic stiffness.

Flexible Bending Arrangement

Individual segments work together as a continuum mechanism. When one segment reaches its angular limit stop, additional cable pull smoothly transfers bending strain to the adjacent segment, producing a continuous, organic curling motion.

1. About Bambu Lab A1 mini

The Bambu Lab A1 mini is a desktop Fused Deposition Modelling (FDM) 3D printer engineered for precision prototyping in academic makerspaces and robotics research laboratories. Utilizing an all-metal hotend and high-performance core architecture, it achieves rapid print speeds of up to 500 mm/s while maintaining tight geometric tolerances required for interlocking mechanical mechanisms.

The system features fully automated sensor-based calibration, including high-frequency eddy-current Flow Dynamics Calibration and multi-point automated bed levelling. This automation ensures repeatable first-layer adhesion, consistent volumetric extrusion, and dimensional accuracy for functional components such as the SPIROBE chassis enclosure, spool drums, and articulated vertebrae.

3. Step-by-Step Wiring Tutorial

Precise electrical termination instructions, logic isolation, and official hardware pin assignments.

7 Engineering Steps
01

Main Power Input & Inline Fuse

Connect the positive lead of the 12V DC power adapter in series with the 5×20mm glass cartridge fuse before connecting to any circuit load. Connect the negative barrel terminal to the common ground junction block.

12V Adapter (+) → Inline Fuse → Positive Distribution Bus
12V Adapter (-) → System Common Ground Rail
02

5V DC-DC Step-Down Converter

Tap the protected +12V positive rail into the SD403 buck converter IN+ terminal, and connect IN- to Common GND. Verify with a multimeter that OUT+ outputs a calibrated, clean 5.0V DC before connecting to the microcontroller.

IN+ ← 12V Protected | IN- ← Common GND
OUT+ → 5.0V Regulated Bus | OUT- → Common GND
03

ESP32 Power Delivery

Route the regulated 5V DC line from the SD403 OUT+ terminal to the ESP32 development board VIN (5V) pin. Connect the adjacent ESP32 GND pin to the common ground rail. Do not connect 5V to the 3.3V pin to avoid damaging the SoC.

5V Regulated Bus → ESP32 VIN (5V Pin)
ESP32 GND Pin → Common Ground Rail
04

L298N Motor Driver Power

Connect the protected 12V rail to the L298N +12V motor supply terminal (VS) and tie the L298N center GND terminal to the system common ground. Feed regulated 5V into the +5V terminal (VSS) if using an external logic rail.

+12V Bus → L298N "+12V" Screw Terminal
Common GND → L298N "GND" Screw Terminal
5V Bus → L298N "+5V" Logic Screw Terminal
05

ESP32 to L298N Control Signals

Connect the ESP32 GPIO pins to the L298N 6-pin logic header using female-to-female Dupont ribbon cables. Maintain clean wire dressing away from high-noise motor leads to prevent signal crosstalk.

Motor A: GPIO 25 (ENA), GPIO 26 (IN1), GPIO 27 (IN2)
Motor B: GPIO 33 (ENB), GPIO 32 (IN3), GPIO 14 (IN4)
06

N20 Motor Terminal Connections

Wire N20 Motor A terminals to L298N blue screw terminals OUT1 and OUT2. Wire N20 Motor B terminals to OUT3 and OUT4. Inverting the wire polarity at the terminal block reverses the winding direction of the respective spool.

OUT1 & OUT2 → N20 Motor A Solder Tabs (Agonist)
OUT3 & OUT4 → N20 Motor B Solder Tabs (Antagonist)
07

Star Common Ground Architecture

Every subsystem (12V adapter negative, SD403 buck converter negative, ESP32 GND pin, and L298N GND terminal) must share a single, unified common ground potential to ensure accurate logic threshold detection and prevent ground loops.

12V (-) == SD403 IN/OUT (-) == ESP32 GND == L298N GND

Official ESP32 to L298N Pin-Mapping Schedule

CRITICAL ELECTRICAL & MOTOR VOLTAGE VERIFICATION
N20 Motor Voltage Management: The N20 micro gearmotors used in this project are nominally rated for 6V DC operation. When driving from a 12V bus through the L298N H-bridge, software PWM duty cycle limits must be clamped (e.g. maximum 50–60% duty cycle) to prevent motor coil overheating and premature brush wear. Software PWM alone does not alter the peak 12V amplitude; verify actual thermal characteristics and current draw on hardware before continuous high-torque duty cycles.

L298N 5V Regulator Jumper Notice: When the 12V motor supply is applied, the onboard 5V regulator jumper enables the internal 78M05 regulator. If an external 5V buck converter (SD403) is used to feed the 5V rail, the jumper setting must be matched to prevent competing regulator cross-feeds. Always verify circuit configuration on hardware.

5. 10-Step Physical Assembly Sequence

Chronological engineering workflow from raw white base plate preparation to final continuity testing.

Step 01
Base Plate Preparation
Inspect and clean the white structural base plate. Verify laser-cut mounting hole coordinates for motor brackets, PCB standoffs, and tentacle root clamp.
Step 02
N20 Motor Mounting
Seat the two N20 geared DC motors horizontally in their custom 3D-printed mounting brackets. Secure brackets to the plate with M3 fasteners.
Step 03
Spool Drum Alignment
Press and align the Part 3 cable winding spools onto the motor D-shafts, ensuring clearance from the base plate and smooth concentric rotation.
Step 04
ESP32 Board Installation
Fasten the ESP32 development board to the upper-central mounting holes using insulated standoffs to prevent trace grounding against the plate.
Step 05
L298N Driver Installation
Mount the L298N motor driver module on the lower-central region, positioning the aluminum heatsink for passive convection airflow.
Step 06
Power Input & Fuse Fitting
Secure the DC barrel screw-terminal jack at the lower-right edge and insert the 5x20mm glass cartridge fuse in series on the positive rail.
Step 07
Buck Converter Integration
Position the SD403 5V step-down regulator module, wire 12V input leads, and calibrate output voltage to 5.00V DC using the onboard trimmer.
Step 08
Control Wire Routing
Route female-to-female Dupont ribbon lines from ESP32 GPIOs (25, 26, 27, 33, 32, 14) to the L298N logic input header, keeping runs neat.
Step 09
Motor Lead Termination
Solder flexible insulated leads to the N20 motor terminal tabs, routing Motor A to OUT1/OUT2 and Motor B to OUT3/OUT4 with strain relief.
Step 10
Continuity Check & Bench Test
Verify star common ground continuity with a digital multimeter, check for power-to-ground shorts, and perform initial Wi-Fi jog testing.

Physical Prototype Diagnosis & Problem Resolution

Engineering Challenges & Sequential Troubleshooting

During high-torque commissioning of the 3D-printed physical prototype, drive-train mechanical slip was encountered. Below is the systematic root-cause isolation and sequential countermeasure implementation verified on the test bench.

2
Spool-to-D-Shaft Coupling Slip White PLA winch spool slipping radially on the motor output D-profile under sustained cable tension.
3
Tendon Cord Anchoring & Knot Slippage Dyneema tendon cord pulling out of spool tie-off eyelet or knot slipping under dynamic shock loading.
Diagnostic Protocol: Inspect with 12V power disconnected. Check closed-cover canopy clearance.
Stage 01

Motor Body Retention & Bracket Deflection

✓ VERIFIED & RESOLVED

Observed Symptom: Micro N20 metal gearbox body experienced angular twist inside the 3D-printed bracket when tendon tension reached maximum holding resistance.

Root Cause Analysis

Smooth nickel-plated motor casing exhibited low friction (μ ≈ 0.15) against printed PLA walls, combined with single-fastener clamp flexure.

Engineering Solution

Added internal anti-rotation knurl ribs to clamp (Part 5 style) + dual M3 bolts with split-lock washers (1.2 N·m clamping torque).

Stage 02

Spool-to-Shaft Mechanical Coupling Slip

✓ VERIFIED & RESOLVED

Observed Symptom: Motor D-shaft rotated continuously during curl command, but winch spool remained stationary under 18.5 N cable tension.

Root Cause Analysis

3mm D-profile bore inside white PLA spool rounded out under high torque; motor coil dissipation accelerated localized plastic creep on the flat face.

Engineering Solution

Integrated a CNC brass threaded insert with an M3 cone-point grub setscrew tightened against the motor shaft flat + Loctite 242 threadlocker.

Stage 03

Tendon Cord Anchoring & Knot Slippage

✓ VERIFIED & RESOLVED

Observed Symptom: Dyneema line slowly unseated from spool anchor hole during repeated 360° curling cycles, causing kinematic zero-point drift.

Root Cause Analysis

Ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) Dyneema fibers have near-zero surface friction; standard single half-hitches untied under cyclic shock loads.

Engineering Solution

Double-pass cross-bored anchor routing with self-cinching figure-eight stopper knot sealed with medical cyanoacrylate (CA) bonding.

Stage 04

Closed-Cover Clearance & Tendon Routing Friction

✓ VERIFIED & RESOLVED

Observed Symptom: Motor stalling and excessive friction occurred only when the top protective chassis canopy (Part 4) was fully fastened onto the baseplate.

Root Cause Analysis

Vertical tolerance stack-up caused tendon lines to rub against internal canopy reinforcement ribs when spool reached full line wrap diameter.

Engineering Solution

Milled 4.5mm chamfered cable relief recesses into canopy underside + installed low-friction PTFE-lined tendon entry guide comb (<0.2 N drag).
